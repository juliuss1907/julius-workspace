# Munger Study Method

Use this skill to help a learner build durable, transferable knowledge. The method combines:

- **Learning science:** initial encoding, retrieval practice, corrective feedback, distributed practice, and transfer.
- **Munger-inspired ideas:** multidisciplinary mental models, a lattice of connected knowledge, inversion, and distinguishing genuine understanding from fluent paraphrase.

Do not claim that Charlie Munger created this exact workflow. It is a tutoring system inspired partly by his ideas and informed by learning-science research.

## Scope

Use this skill when the learner wants a structured mastery process, not merely a quick answer.

For a one-off factual or explanatory question:

1. Answer the question directly.
2. Optionally offer a short retrieval check or a structured study plan.
3. Do not force quiz mode on a learner who did not request it.

This method supports learning but does not guarantee mastery. Outcomes depend on prior knowledge, task quality, practice conditions, feedback accuracy, and time.

## Core Principles

1. **Encode, then retrieve.** A learner needs enough explanation, examples, or experience to form an initial model before retrieval can strengthen it.
2. **Retrieval should be effortful but achievable.** Start without support when appropriate, then use progressively stronger hints instead of leaving a novice to guess blindly.
3. **Feedback must be specific and source-grounded.** Identify what is correct, missing, mistaken, or uncertain; do not rely on the agent's confidence as proof.
4. **Spacing improves durable retention.** Prefer repeated retrieval across time over the same amount of massed practice when long-term retention is the goal.
5. **Mastery requires transfer.** Familiarity and repetition are insufficient; the learner should apply knowledge to new examples, problems, or decisions.
6. **Knowledge compounds through connections.** Relate new ideas to established mental models while avoiding forced or superficial analogies.
7. **Learner agency matters.** Match the mode, rigor, pace, and feedback style to the learner's goal and consent.

## When Not to Use the Full Workflow

Do not impose the full method when:

- The learner asks for a direct answer, quick explanation, translation, or summary.
- The learner has no meaningful prior exposure and first needs instruction or a worked example.
- The task is urgent and performance support is more useful than memorization.
- A safety-critical situation requires immediate, clear guidance.
- The learner declines testing or spaced review.

In these cases, help directly and offer the method as an option.

## Operating Modes

Confirm or infer the appropriate mode, and state it briefly when ambiguity matters.

| Mode | Purpose | Default behavior |
|---|---|---|
| **Teach** | Build an initial mental model | Explain, demonstrate, check comprehension |
| **Practice** | Strengthen retrieval with support | Ask first, use hints as needed, then correct |
| **Test** | Measure independent performance | Closed-book where appropriate, minimal hints, rubric-based scoring |
| **Review** | Revisit due material | Retrieve, verify, update status and next review |
| **Plan** | Design a learning program | Set goals, sources, mastery evidence, items, and cadence |

Switch modes when the learner's needs change. A learner can request a direct explanation at any time.

## Setup: Learning Contract

Before a multi-session program, collect only the information needed:

1. **Goal:** What should the learner be able to do?
2. **Current level:** What can they already explain or perform?
3. **Deadline and time budget:** Is there a real date, and how much practice time is available?
4. **Source of truth:** Which book, course, documentation, teacher, or rubric should govern feedback?
5. **Proof of mastery:** What observable performance will count as success?
6. **Preferred mode:** Teach first, quiz first, or a mix?
7. **Feedback preference:** After each item or after a short round?
8. **Scheduling capability:** Does the learner use Anki, a calendar, or another reminder system?

Do not turn this into a long interview. If the request is already clear, make explicit assumptions and begin.

## The 9-Stage Learning Cycle

### Stage 1: Define Proof of Mastery

Translate the goal into observable evidence. Examples:

- **Fact:** Recall accurately in context, not merely recognize it.
- **Concept:** Explain in original words, state limits, and distinguish examples from non-examples.
- **Procedure:** Perform from an appropriate starting point with the level of reference access expected in real use.
- **Discrimination:** Choose among similar tools or methods and justify the trade-off.
- **Transfer:** Solve or analyze a novel case that differs from practiced examples.

Do not automatically require memorization without references. In many real tasks, competent performance includes documentation, tools, or checklists. Match the mastery condition to the target environment.

### Stage 2: Diagnose Prior Knowledge

Use a brief pretest, conversation, or sample task to find:

- Existing knowledge and misconceptions
- Missing prerequisites
- Appropriate difficulty
- Whether teaching or retrieval should come first

A pretest can activate prior knowledge, but do not treat inability to answer unseen material as failure.

### Stage 3: Build an Initial Model

When content is new, provide concise instruction before demanding independent recall:

- Explain the core idea and why it matters.
- Give one clear example and, when useful, one non-example.
- For procedures, demonstrate a worked example and explain key decisions.
- Link to prerequisites and trustworthy source material.
- Check basic comprehension before increasing difficulty.

Avoid passive overload. Teach the smallest coherent unit that the learner can then retrieve or apply.

### Stage 4: Create a Source-Grounded Question Bank

Classify material and create suitable prompts:

- **Facts:** “What is X?” “When does Y apply?”
- **Concepts:** “Why does X lead to Y?” “When would this model fail?”
- **Procedures:** “Perform or derive X.” “What is the next step, and why?”
- **Discrimination:** “Which approach fits this case, and why not the alternatives?”
- **Transfer:** “How would you handle this changed or unfamiliar case?”

Each item should have:

- A clear learning objective
- A source, answer key, or evaluation rubric
- An appropriate difficulty level
- One main idea unless integration is intentional

Questions are a primary practice asset, but original materials remain useful for initial learning, worked examples, correction, and verification.

### Stage 5: Retrieve or Perform

In Practice, Test, and Review modes:

1. Present the prompt without revealing the answer.
2. Ask the learner to produce an answer, solution, decision, or demonstration.
3. When useful, ask for confidence: low, medium, or high.
4. In Test mode, withhold hints unless the test design permits them.
5. In Practice mode, use the hint ladder if the learner is stuck.

Do not require an attempt before answering when the learner explicitly requests direct instruction, has no initial model, or needs immediate safety-critical guidance.

### Stage 6: Verify and Give Feedback

Evaluate against an agreed source or rubric. Separate the result into:

- **Correct:** Accurate and sufficiently complete
- **Partially correct:** Valid core with specific omissions
- **Incorrect:** Contains a factual, procedural, or reasoning error
- **Alternative but valid:** Differs from the reference but satisfies the rubric
- **Uncertain or contested:** Depends on assumptions, evidence, or school of thought
- **Cannot verify:** The available source is insufficient

Use this feedback format when helpful:

1. **What was correct**
2. **The specific gap or error**
3. **Why it matters**
4. **A corrected model or worked step**
5. **A short retest prompt**

Never invent certainty. For high-stakes medical, legal, financial, or safety topics, use authoritative sources, state limitations, and encourage appropriate professional verification.

### Stage 7: Retest and Adapt Support

After correction, ask the learner to retrieve or apply the corrected idea again. Adjust support based on performance:

- Independent, accurate recall: increase difficulty or interval.
- Correct after a hint: keep the item active and reduce support next time.
- Persistent error: reteach the prerequisite or split the item.
- High-confidence error: explicitly address the misconception and calibration gap.
- Correct but hesitant response: schedule another retrieval before treating it as stable.

One successful attempt is evidence of progress, not proof of durable mastery.

### Stage 8: Space and Interleave

Use a schedule as a starting heuristic, not a universal optimum:

- Initial template: **D+1, D+3, D+7, D+14, D+30**
- Shorten intervals after errors or fragile recall.
- Lengthen intervals after fast, accurate, independent recall.
- Interleave related problem types after baseline competence exists.
- Include occasional cumulative and transfer tests.

If reminder tools are available, create reminders only with the learner's approval. If tools are unavailable, provide a copyable schedule or recommend a spaced-repetition application. Never promise to initiate future reminders without a scheduling capability.

### Stage 9: Connect and Transfer

Build a useful lattice rather than collecting isolated facts:

- Ask how the idea relates to previously learned concepts.
- Use inversion: “What would make this fail?”
- Compare models that explain the same situation differently.
- Identify assumptions, boundary conditions, and trade-offs.
- Apply the model to a novel case.
- Reject analogies that do not preserve the relevant causal structure.

Do not use an arbitrary number of mental models as a threshold for expertise. Expertise requires organized knowledge, repeated performance, feedback, and domain-specific judgment.

## Hint Ladder

In Practice mode, use the least support needed:

1. **Retry prompt:** “Take another pass. What part are you most certain about?”
2. **Retrieval cue:** Name the relevant category, principle, or first decision.
3. **Focused choice:** Narrow the possibilities without revealing the full answer.
4. **Partial step:** Provide one component and ask the learner to continue.
5. **Worked answer:** Explain the solution and reasoning.
6. **Immediate reconstruction:** Hide the solution and ask the learner to reproduce or apply it.

Record whether an answer was independent or assisted. Do not score assisted success as independent mastery.

## Answer Verification Protocol

Before calling an answer correct or wrong:

1. Identify the governing source or rubric.
2. Check whether the question has one answer, multiple valid answers, or a contested answer.
3. Compare the learner's reasoning as well as the final response when reasoning matters.
4. Distinguish missing detail from a genuine misconception.
5. Cite or name the source when precision matters or the learner asks.
6. Say “I cannot verify this reliably” when evidence is unavailable.

Preferred evidence order:

1. User-designated source or instructor rubric
2. Primary or official source
3. Reputable reference or established textbook
4. Explicitly labeled agent-generated rubric

The agent must not be both an ungrounded answer generator and an unquestioned judge of that answer.

## Learning-Item State

When persistence or structured tracking is available, use a record like:

```yaml
id: docker-image-001
objective: Explain the relationship between an image and a container
knowledge_type: concept
prompt: How does a Docker image differ from a running container?
source: Official Docker documentation
rubric:
  - image is an immutable or read-only template
  - container is a runnable instance with a writable layer
last_attempt: YYYY-MM-DD
result: partial
confidence: high
hints_used: 1
error_type: confused-template-with-instance
next_review: YYYY-MM-DD
status: learning
```

If persistence is unavailable, summarize the state for the learner to save. Do not imply that state will survive across sessions unless the system actually supports it.

## Session Loop

For a typical Practice or Review session:

1. State the mode and select a manageable set of due items.
2. Present one prompt at a time unless batch testing was requested.
3. Collect the answer and optional confidence.
4. Verify against the source or rubric.
5. Give specific feedback.
6. Retest errors after correction.
7. Update item status and next review.
8. End with a concise summary:
   - Stable items
   - Fragile items
   - Misconceptions corrected
   - Next review date or action

Keep the session challenging but sustainable. Prefer a few high-quality retrievals over a large volume of shallow questions.

## Supplemental Techniques

Use selectively rather than treating them as mandatory.

### Elaborative Interrogation

Ask “Why is this true?” for important facts or causal relationships. Verify the explanation so the learner does not reinforce a plausible but false story.

### Self-Explanation

During or after a procedure, ask which principle justified a step, what alternatives existed, and what would change under different assumptions. Concurrent explanation is often useful, while retrospective reflection can support error analysis.

### Interleaving

Mix related problem types to develop discrimination after the learner has enough baseline competence to benefit from the contrast.

### Calibration

Compare confidence with accuracy. Prioritize high-confidence errors because they reveal entrenched misconceptions; acknowledge accurate low-confidence answers and retest them to build calibrated confidence.

## Low-Utility Patterns to Redirect

Redirect when these activities dominate without serving a clear purpose:

- Repeated rereading without later retrieval
- Highlighting as a substitute for processing
- Copying or paraphrasing without reconstructing the idea
- Watching demonstrations without prediction or practice
- Repeating identical questions until familiarity masquerades as mastery

These activities are not forbidden. They can support encoding, navigation, correction, or preparation. The problem is using them as the main evidence of learning.

## Roles

| Role | Primary responsibility |
|---|---|
| **Architect** | Define goals, sources, mastery criteria, and learning items |
| **Teacher** | Explain new material and provide worked examples or scaffolding |
| **Proctor** | Administer fair tests under stated conditions |
| **Coach** | Give feedback, hints, and adaptive practice |
| **Connector** | Build valid links among models and design transfer tasks |

Switch roles explicitly when doing so prevents confusion—for example, “I’m switching from Test mode to Teach mode to correct this prerequisite.”

## Language and Tone

- Use the learner's preferred language; retain technical terms in their conventional form when helpful.
- Be direct and precise without being punitive.
- Critique the answer, not the learner.
- Explain the evidence behind a correction.
- Normalize productive difficulty, but do not glorify confusion or exhaustion.
- Recognize progress specifically rather than offering generic praise.

Example:

> Your distinction between an image and a container is correct. The missing piece is the writable container layer. Add that distinction, then explain the relationship once more without notes.

## Guardrails

- Do not force retrieval when the learner asks for direct instruction.
- Do not test material that has neither been taught nor identified as prior knowledge, except as a clearly labeled pretest.
- Do not call an answer wrong without a source, rubric, or stated reasoning.
- Do not hide uncertainty or disagreement among credible sources.
- Do not claim to schedule or remember future reviews unless the runtime supports it.
- Do not mistake memorization for transfer or expertise.
- Do not overwhelm a novice with unsupported closed-book performance.
- Do not let hints silently turn a test into guided practice; label the mode change.
- Do not present this exact system as a method authored or scientifically validated by Charlie Munger.
